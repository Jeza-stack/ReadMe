  criticalAnalysis: >-
    The poem's genius lies in its perversity — it teaches how to destroy
    something in order to make us understand how remarkable that thing's
    survival truly is. By focusing on what it takes to kill, Patel celebrates
    what it takes to live. The tree emerges as the most resilient and admirable
    character in modern Indian poetry.
  relevance: >-
    The poem speaks simultaneously to environmental destruction, colonial
    history, personal resilience, and the perseverance required to survive
    systemic attacks. Students across disciplines find it endlessly
    interpretable — its medical precision and philosophical depth make it ideal

    answer: >-
      The tree has been read as a symbol of the human spirit, colonised peoples,
      rooted cultures, or any living system with deep historical roots —

  - question: Why is hacking the tree not enough to kill it?
    answer: >-
      The tree has built resilience through years of absorbing pain — the
      bleeding bark heals, new shoots rise from near the ground. Only destroying
      the root — the source — prevents regeneration.
  - question: What role does the word 'absorbing' play in the poem?
    answer: >-
      Absorbing appears twice — the tree absorbs sunlight to grow and absorbs
      shocks of pain to survive. This repetition reveals that the tree's
      extraordinary strength comes from its capacity to metabolise both
      nourishment and suffering.
  - question: Is this an environmentalist poem or does it carry a political reading?
    answer: >-
      On the surface it describes deforestation with disturbing precision.
      Allegorically, critics read it as describing oppression — the methodical
      destruction required to truly eliminate a people or culture with deep
      historical roots.

It takes much time to kill a tree,
Not a simple jab of the knife
Will do it. It has grown
Slowly consuming the earth,
Rising out of it, feeding
Upon its crust, absorbing
Years of sunlight, air, water,
And out of its leprous hide
Sprouting leaves.

So hack and chop
But this alone won't do it.
Not so much pain will do it.
The bleeding bark will heal
And from close to the ground
Will rise curled green twigs,
Absorbing shocks of pain,
Twisting, adapting, hardening—
Leaping out of the Earth.

Then the matter
Of scorching and choking
In sun and air,
Browning, hardening,
Twisting, withering,
And then it is done.
